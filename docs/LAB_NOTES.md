# Lab notes: how the four agent roles are executed here

The prompt architecture in `Hex-Nexus_MultiAgent_Prompts.md` defines four agents.
This repository is the artefact trail they produce. Each role's outputs live in
the directory it owns (Prompts 2.4), and every role follows its own prompt:

| role | owns | Phase 0 output |
|---|---|---|
| Facilitator | `rules/`, `log/`, `reports/requests/` | RULE-Q rulings (26), the changelog, sim requests for batches 0001-0003, the iteration summary |
| Champion & Rule Designer | `roster/` | `roster_v1.0.0.json`: 10 champions, 2 per role, each on budget with a worksheet |
| Champion AI Developer | `ai/` | `policy_v1_0_0`: T0_random, T1_greedy, shared evaluation function |
| Monte Carlo Sim Runner | `engine/`, `tests/`, `tools/`, `reports/` | engine + 76 unit tests + batch runner + Part 3.5 reports |

## Engine design notes (Sim Runner)

**The tile graph.** Rules 3.1 and 4.1 make a hidden tile a single space. The
engine's movement and range graph therefore has two node kinds: `("H", q, r)`
for a hex in a face-up tile and `("T", t)` for a whole face-down tile. Every
distance, range and adjacency query runs over that graph, so "1 step into a
hidden tile, 1 step out" and "range 1 from every bordering hex" fall out for
free. Neighbour lookups are memoised on the hidden-tile bitmask.

**Visibility is recomputed, not tracked by hand.** A tile must be face up while
both teams are inside it (Rules 3.2). `refresh_visibility` derives that from the
unit positions at the checkpoints Rules 3.5 names — after every activation and
after minion movement — plus an immediate flip when a mover bumps into an
occupied tile (Rules 3.3). Control Wards and REVEAL pin a tile face up.

**Nominal hexes.** A champion inside a hidden tile has no individual hex
(Rules 3.5), but the engine still stores one so the unit index stays uniform.
The flip procedure hands out real hexes, with the owner's policy choosing the
assignment and overflow spilling outside the tile (Rules 3.3-3.4).

**Legal options only.** Every decision point is generated by the engine:
activations (movement destination, recall, flip entry, ability, targets, Swift
Tonic), flip placements, card plays and purchases. Policies only choose. Two
enumeration limits are documented in every report: options per activation are
capped (movement, recall and flip-entry options are never dropped), and a step's
target list is read from the state before the ability resolves.

**Assertions.** After every activation, every World Phase and every Upkeep the
engine checks stacking, HP range, cooldown positions, chip counts and the
"no hidden tile holds both teams" invariant. A batch with any illegal state is
reported as invalid. Two real bugs were caught this way in batch_0001 and fixed
before the baseline was taken (monster respawn onto an occupied hex, and a wave
spawning under a friendly champion), plus one found by the same assertions in
the test suite (a tile turning face up mid-ability invalidating a move target).

## AI design notes (AI Developer)

`T0_random` chooses uniformly among legal options but never passes up a free
farm (L0 on a wave). It is the competence floor, not a player.

`T1_greedy` applies each legal activation to a cloned state, scores the result
with a single shared evaluation function, and picks with a softmax at the
requested temperature. The evaluation weighs: structure chips on both sides, AP
in the pool, champion HP and lives, predicted World Phase damage for and against
(so it respects tower dives), death risk and kill chance, cooldown exposure
(Rules 5.2: a champion on cooldown cannot even move), wave chips and wave push,
Dragon and Baron, and a small lane-assignment term by role. Per-champion
knowledge is limited to the role hint table, per Prompts 4.C.

## Phase 1: T2_search and the exploit set (ai 1.1.0)

`T2_search` subclasses the greedy policy and adds a depth-limited look-ahead
over the snake order, which the engine now publishes on the state
(`turn_order`, `turn_index`) so the search knows whether the next activation is
the opponent's or its own second slot.

Each decision: prefilter every legal activation with the greedy score, expand
the best `root_k` of them, reply with a T1 model of the opponent built on a
tightened enumeration config, and evaluate the leaf. A per-decision time budget
bounds the whole thing. Two properties are deliberate:

* **Scores never mix scales.** A searched value already contains the
  opponent's best reply, so it is systematically lower than a greedy one.
  Running out of time shrinks the candidate set; it never lets an unsearched
  score compete with a searched one. Passing is searched the same way.
* **`leaf_world` resolves the World Phase at the leaf.** Damage in this game
  lands in the World Phase (Rules 5.3), so a leaf that has not resolved it
  misreads every tower dive and minion trade. When it is on, the leaf is
  evaluated with the predicted-damage terms zeroed, because the damage is now
  real rather than predicted.

Macro planning lives in the weight profile, not in bespoke code: lane and
jungle assignment by role, a pull toward a live Dragon or Baron for the
jungler, and recall value for a hurt champion sitting in its own base. Shop
builds by role are inherited from T1.

The six `X_exploit_*` policies are the same greedy policy with a distorted
weight profile each - dive, farm-and-scale, split-push, objective hoarding,
turtle and cooldown-lock. Because they share the evaluator, an exploit that
beats T2 is a statement about the rules rather than about bespoke AI code.

`tools/ai_calibrate.py` runs head-to-head variants (the variant always takes
the first-priority seat, and seats swap every other game), and
`tools/ai_acceptance.py` grades the Prompts 4.C checks into one report.

## What the Phase 1 calibration found

Ten T2 variants were run against T1 (`reports/calibration/plan_a..d`): macro
weights alone, baseline weights, a wider root, a finer opponent model, the
World Phase resolved at the leaf, a sharper softmax, and a full round rollout.
Pooled over the first 540 games the T2 family beat T1 **56.5% [52.3, 60.6]**,
and the best single configuration reached **62.0% [54.0, 69.4]**. Every variant
landed between 52% and 62%; nothing came close to jumping the 65% gate.

`tools/search_agreement.py` then asked the question that separates an AI
problem from a game problem: **how often does the searched best move differ
from the greedy best?** Over 399 decisions in 12 games, the searched choice
differed **45.4%** of the time, and when it differed it gave up an average of
**4.1 points** of immediate greedy value to do so.

So the search is not failing to find different moves - it finds them
constantly, pays real immediate value for them, and wins only a few points more
often. The reading that fits: in Hex-Nexus a large share of activations are
close to value-neutral. Two very different plans for the same champion end the
round in much the same place, because one activation is one champion of five,
damage lands in a separate World Phase, and the whole card goes on cooldown
either way. That is a statement about the game's decision structure, and it
belongs in front of the lead designer rather than in the AI backlog.
